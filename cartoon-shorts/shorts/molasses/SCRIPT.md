# The Day Boston Drowned in Syrup

Format: story mode. One event (the 1919 Boston molasses flood), one mechanism: a leaky tank built by an
accountant, never tested and painted brown to hide the leaks, bursts on a warm day. The twist ending is that
the molasses was for making alcohol, and the next day America approved the ban on alcohol.

**Hook (owner's rule):** frame 0 is mid-flood. A giant brown wave crashes down the street, and people run at
the camera shouting while it sweeps them up and carries them along. Cartoon style, nobody shown hurt. A crowd
scream and a wave roar play on frame 0, and the full claim is spoken by about 2.5 s.

**Humour rule:** laugh at the company's foolishness, never at the victims. The 21 deaths are stated once, plainly.

## Facts (2+ sources each)
- 15 Jan 1919, about 12:30 pm, Commercial Street, North End, Boston. Purity Distilling, owned by US Industrial Alcohol (USIA). Sources: Wikipedia "Great Molasses Flood"; Britannica.
- The tank was 50 ft tall and 90 ft wide and held about 2.3 million gallons (sources say 2 to 2.3 million). Sources: Wikipedia; Britannica; WorldAtlas.
- The wave moved at about 35 mph and stood about 25 ft high (estimates run 15 to 40 ft). Sources: Wikipedia; Britannica; Boston 25 News.
- Rivets popped out with a sound like a machine gun. Steel hit the elevated railway's girders, a streetcar was knocked off its tracks, and a truck was thrown into the harbor. Source: Wikipedia, quoting witnesses.
- One boy, Anthony di Stasio, rode the crest of the wave and survived. Source: Wikipedia, quoting Edwards Park in Smithsonian, 1983.
- 21 people died and about 150 were hurt. Sources: Wikipedia; Britannica.
- Arthur Jell, the company treasurer, oversaw the tank's construction with no engineering experience and could not read blueprints. Sources: Wikipedia; Straight Dope; Dying Words.
- The tank was never properly tested: its water test was too shallow to show leaks. Sources: Wikipedia; Chemical Processing ("leakage tests ... never performed").
- It leaked from the start and was painted brown to hide the leaks. Locals collected the leaking molasses to take home. Sources: Wikipedia; Dying Words / Straight Dope ("painted brown").
- It was a warm day: the temperature had risen from 2 °F to above 40 °F. Sources: Wikipedia; Britannica.
- Rescuers waded in, and the syrup thickened as it cooled. "Everything that a Bostonian touched was sticky": subway seats, streetcar seats, payphones. Sources: Wikipedia; Boston 25 News.
- USIA blamed anarchists. A court-appointed auditor found the company responsible after three years of hearings, and the company paid $628,000. Sources: Wikipedia; History Skills; Britannica.
- The harbor stayed brown until summer. Sources: Wikipedia; Atlas Obscura.
- People said the area smelled of molasses for decades. Sources: Wikipedia, quoting Edwards Park; WGBH.
- The molasses was fermented into alcohol. The 18th Amendment (Prohibition) was ratified on 16 Jan 1919, the day after the flood. Sources: Wikipedia; Britannica.
- Staging only: the bucket line of locals and the brown paint can are visual gags drawn from the facts.

Script:
A tidal wave of syrup flooded Boston.
Nineteen nineteen. Thirty-five miles an hour.
It started with one giant tank. Fifty feet tall, full of molasses.
The man in charge of building it was not an engineer. He was the company's accountant.
The accountant could not even read the blueprints.
He never tested it properly, so it leaked from day one.
His solution? He painted the tank brown.
So the leaks would match.
The neighbors just showed up with buckets, and took the free molasses home.
Then came one warm day in January.
The tank started groaning.
Rivets popped out like machine-gun fire.
And two million gallons came out at once.
The wave knocked a streetcar off its tracks and threw a truck into the harbor.
One boy rode the top of it, like a surfer.
Twenty-one people died. Rescuers rushed in, and the cold turned the syrup into glue.
The whole city got sticky. Subway seats. Payphones. Everything.
The company's excuse? A bomb, planted by anarchists.
Three years of hearings later, the court said no. It was just a bad tank.
The harbor stayed brown until summer, and the streets smelled sweet for decades.
And what was all that molasses for? Making alcohol.
The very next day, America approved the law that banned alcohol.
