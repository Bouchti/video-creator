# The Bribe That Sold the Eiffel Tower

Format: story mode (viral-format research, 2026-11-05). One con, one mechanism, twist ending, no viewer question.

## Facts (sources)
- 1925, Paris: Victor Lustig read a newspaper piece on how costly the tower was to maintain and repair; it was built
  for the 1889 World's Fair and was never meant to stay (Eiffel's permit ran 20 years, to 1909).
  Wikipedia "Victor Lustig"; amusingplanet.com/2022/06/the-man-who-sold-eiffel-towertwice.html
- He posed as Deputy Director-General of the Ministère des Postes et Télégraphes, had fake government stationery made,
  and met five scrap-metal dealers at the Hôtel de Crillon, asking for total discretion (public outcry).
  Wikipedia; amusingplanet; thehustle.co "The con man who sold the Eiffel Tower — twice"
- He took the dealers on a tour of the tower in a rented limousine (thehustle.co; isri.org "Selling the Eiffel Tower").
- He picked André Poisson, "an insecure man who wished to rise up amongst the inner circles of the Parisian business
  community" (Wikipedia). Poisson's wife was suspicious (common telling, e.g. todayifoundout.com).
- Lustig "confessed" his government salary didn't cover his lifestyle; Poisson took it as a bribe request from a real,
  corrupt official, paid the bribe and the sale money (amusingplanet; Wikipedia: "his bribe and the funds").
- Lustig fled to Austria; Poisson was too ashamed to go to the police (Wikipedia; Lustig's own expectation).
- About a month later he came back to Paris and tried to sell the tower again, to new dealers; that one went to the
  police and Lustig escaped (Wikipedia; social-engineer.org; amusingplanet).
- Staging only (not claimed as fact): knocking on girders, the newspaper-checking montage.

## Title
The Bribe That Sold the Eiffel Tower

Script:
He sold the Eiffel Tower for scrap metal, and the buyer paid him a bribe on top.
It's Paris, nineteen twenty-five, and the papers say the tower is rusting, costs a fortune to repaint, and was only ever meant to stand for twenty years.
So he has a forger print fake government letterhead, and invites five scrap dealers to the fanciest hotel in town.
He tells them he's from the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs.
"The post office is selling the Eiffel Tower?"
"Shh. If Paris finds out, there will be riots. Not a word."
He even takes them on a tour in a limousine, knocking on the girders like he's checking a melon.
One dealer, André Poisson, wants it more than anyone. He's an outsider, and owning the tower would make him the biggest name in Paris.
But his wife isn't buying it. "A secret deal, in a hotel, in a hurry? Something's off."
So the dealer is ready to walk away. And that's when the con man asks him for a bribe.
Wait, a bribe? Wouldn't that blow his cover?
You see, everyone in Paris knew government officials took bribes. A fake one would never dare to ask.
So the moment he holds out his hand, the dealer relaxes. Only a real official would be that crooked.
He pays the bribe and the price of the tower, and the con man is on a train to Austria with a suitcase full of cash.
Every morning he checks the papers for his face. Nothing.
The dealer is so embarrassed, he never tells the police.
So a month later, the con man comes back to Paris, to sell the Eiffel Tower again.
