# "Where Do Escalator Steps Go?" — original script v1 (2026-11-04, user pre-approved: "don't wait for my approval")
Format: cartoon 3D explainer short, > 60 s (target 63-68 s), one steady narrator, no captions, ends with a viewer question.

Facts:
- Harrods, Brompton Road, London: England's first "moving staircase", 16 November 1898 — really an inclined woven
  leather conveyor belt (224 linked pieces) with a mahogany and silver plate-glass balustrade. Staff reportedly offered
  free brandy / cognac and smelling salts at the top to shaken shoppers (contemporary press; "reportedly").
  (walks.com "Today in London History, Nov 16"; thevintagenews.com 2017 "first escalator"; Wikipedia "Harrods")
- A modern escalator: steps on two step chains (one each side) driven by a sprocket at the top from the drive motor;
  each step has two step-chain rollers (front) and two trailer rollers (rear) on separate tracks; on the incline the
  tracks keep the tread horizontal, at the landings the tracks level out and the steps flatten; at the top the steps
  turn round the drive sprocket and return upside down underneath; the comb plate's teeth mesh with the step cleats.
- Handrail: a rubber loop driven by the same drive; EN 115-1: handrail speed tolerance 0 % to +2 % of the step speed
  (never slower). (IS 17900 / EN 115-1:2017 comparison; Messe Frankfurt interview)
- "Escalator" registered by Charles Seeberger in 1900, assigned to Otis; cancelled 1950 as generic
  (Haughton Elevator Co. v. Seeberger, 85 USPQ 80, Comm'r 1950), the decision citing Otis's own generic use.
Analogy: a bicycle chain (one loop round two gears).

Script:
Every escalator step you've ever ridden slides into the floor, flips upside down, and sneaks back underneath you.
It's 1898, and Harrods, London's fanciest store, unveils England's first moving staircase.
It's really a leather belt, sloping up between polished wooden rails.
Shoppers are so shaken that staff reportedly wait at the top with brandy and smelling salts.
Luckily, nobody fell. Unluckily, the brandy was free.
Today's escalator is one big loop, like a bicycle chain.
A motor turns a big gear at the top, and two chains drag every step around it.
Each step rolls on two wheels in front and two behind, on two separate tracks.
On the slope, the back track rides higher, so every step stays flat, like a stair.
At the ends, both tracks level out, and the stairs melt into a flat floor.
Those grooves on every step line up with a metal comb, so shoelaces get pushed out, not pulled in.
Then the step tips over the gear and rides back down, upside down, right under your shoes.
The handrail is one giant rubber loop, pulled by the same motor.
And Europe's safety rules say it can run up to two percent faster than the steps, but never slower.
The word escalator was once a brand name. Otis lost it in 1950, because everyone, even Otis, used it as a normal word.
So next time you ride one, watch your hand. Is it creeping ahead of your feet?
