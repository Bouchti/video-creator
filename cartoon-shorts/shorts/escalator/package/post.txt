Where Do Escalator Steps Go When They Disappear? #Shorts

DESCRIPTION:
An escalator is one big loop, like a bicycle chain. A motor turns a gear at the top, and two chains pull every step around it. Each step rolls on two wheels in front and two behind, on two separate tracks: on the slope the back track rides higher so the step stays flat like a stair, and at the ends both tracks level out so the stairs melt into a flat floor. The grooves on each step slide between the teeth of a metal comb, then the step tips over the gear and rides back down upside down, right under your shoes. The handrail is a separate rubber loop driven by the same motor.

In 1898 Harrods in London unveiled England's first "moving staircase": really a woven leather belt between mahogany rails. Staff reportedly waited at the top with brandy and smelling salts for nervous shoppers. "Escalator" was once a trademark; it was ruled a generic word in 1950, partly because Otis used it that way itself. European safety rules (EN 115) let the handrail run up to 2 % faster than the steps, never slower.
