## Facts (with sources)
- **EUV light source** ([ASML, "The hottest thing about ASML machines"](https://www.asml.com/en/discover/hot-machines)):
  - Tin droplets shoot out at 70 m/s.
  - A first laser pulse flattens each droplet, and a second, stronger pulse turns it into plasma at about 500,000 K, "hotter than the surface of the sun".
  - This happens 50,000 times every second.
  - The light is 13.5 nm EUV. Heise also reports 50,000 droplets/s with two shots per droplet.
- **EUV needs a vacuum:** air absorbs EUV light, so the light path runs in vacuum, with multilayer mirrors and no lenses. Source: ASML, same page.
- **Mirrors** ([Zeiss's Peter Kürz, World of Photonics interview](https://world-of-photonics.com/en/newsroom/photonics-industry-portal/photonics-interview/dr-peter-kuerz/)): "If one of our EUV mirrors were the size of Germany, the largest deviations from the ideal form would be only 0.1 mm." *Chip War* (Miller) gives the same figure.
- **Machine size and delivery** ([Intel Newsroom, "EUV: The Most Precise, Complex Machine at Intel"](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/newsroom/news/euv-most-precise-complex-machine.html)): 100,000 parts and nearly 200 tons. Delivering one tool takes "three Boeing 747 cargo planes, 40 freight containers and 20 trucks".
- **Only one maker:** ASML is the only company that builds EUV lithography machines. Sources: Reuters explainer, Jan 2026; Silicon Canals.
- **First transistor** ([Wikipedia, "Point-contact transistor"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point-contact_transistor); [Invention & Technology](https://www.inventionandtech.com/)):
  - Bardeen and Brattain built it at Bell Labs, Murray Hill, New Jersey, in December 1947 (the 16th; it was demonstrated on the 23rd).
  - It was gold foil contacts on a germanium block, pressed down by a spring, with a plastic wedge between them.
  - It was "quarter-inch-tall" and replaced vacuum tubes.
- **Newspaper coverage:** Bell Labs held a press conference on June 30 1948. The New York Times ran a few paragraphs on page 46 on July 1 1948, at the end of the column "The News of Radio". Sources: [IEEE-USA Insight](https://insight.ieeeusa.org/), [Linda Hall Library](https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/the-transistor/).
- **The phone chip:** Apple's A18 Pro (iPhone 16 Pro) has about 20 billion transistors on TSMC 3 nm, and its die is about the size of a fingernail. World population is about 8.2 billion, so the chip has more than twice as many switches. Sources: [Wikipedia, "Apple A18"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_A18), Digitimes.
- **From sand to wafer:** quartz sand is refined to electronic-grade silicon, about 1 foreign atom per billion (9N). It is grown into a Czochralski single crystal, sliced into wafers and polished mirror-smooth. Sources: Intel "How a CPU is made" (Tom's Hardware); MicroChemicals app note.
- **Yellow cleanroom light:** photoresist reacts to light from about 200 to 500 nm, so lithography bays use yellow light with no blue or UV. Sources: MPG HLL lithography page; MIT MTL labnetwork.

Script:
The chip inside your phone was printed with light, made by blasting falling drops of tin with a laser, fifty thousand times a second.
It began in nineteen forty-seven, at Bell Labs in New Jersey, with one tiny switch called a transistor.
It was a strip of gold foil, pressed into a chunk of germanium by a bent spring.
When Bell Labs showed it off, the New York Times put the story on page forty-six.
Luckily, it changed the world. Unluckily, it was tucked inside the radio column.
Today, your phone's chip holds about twenty billion of those switches, more than twice the number of people on Earth.
It starts as sand, cleaned until only one atom in a billion is something else.
That silicon grows into one giant crystal, sliced into mirror-shiny wafers.
Then the switches are printed on, layer by layer, like shining light through a stencil.
That's why chip factories glow yellow: blue light would spoil the coating before it's printed.
But these switches are so tiny that normal light is too blunt. So the machine makes its own.
A drop of tin falls, a laser flattens it, and a second blast turns it into plasma, hotter than the surface of the Sun.
Air would swallow that light, so it travels through a vacuum, bouncing off mirrors.
They're so smooth that, grown to the size of Germany, the biggest bump would be a tenth of a millimetre.
The machine weighs nearly two hundred tons, and it's flown to the factory in three jumbo jets.
And only one company on Earth can build it.
So every time you tap your screen, twenty billion tiny switches flip. How many did you flip today?
