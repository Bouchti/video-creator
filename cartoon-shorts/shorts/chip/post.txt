TITLE (max 100 chars):
Your Phone's Chip Was Printed With Exploding Tin #Shorts

DESCRIPTION:
In 1947 at Bell Labs, Bardeen and Brattain built the first transistor: a strip of gold foil pressed into a chunk of germanium by a bent spring. When Bell Labs showed it off in 1948, the New York Times put the story on page 46, inside "The News of Radio" column.

Today a phone chip like Apple's A18 Pro holds about 20 billion transistors, more than twice the number of people on Earth. Chips start as sand, refined until only about one atom in a billion is something else. The silicon is grown into one giant crystal and sliced into mirror-smooth wafers. Then the switches are printed on, layer by layer, like light through a stencil. That is why chip factories glow yellow: the light-sensitive coating reacts to blue and UV light.

The smallest patterns are printed with extreme ultraviolet light (13.5 nm). A drop of tin falls, a laser pulse flattens it, and a second, stronger pulse turns it into plasma hotter than the surface of the Sun. This happens 50,000 times a second. Air would absorb that light, so it travels through a vacuum, bouncing off mirrors so smooth that, grown to the size of Germany, the biggest bump would be about 0.1 mm. One machine weighs nearly 200 tons and is delivered in three Boeing 747 cargo planes. Only one company on Earth builds them.

So every time you tap your screen, billions of tiny switches flip. How many did you flip today? 👇
