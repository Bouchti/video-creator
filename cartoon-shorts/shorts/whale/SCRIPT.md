# Oregon Blew Up a Whale. On Purpose.

Format: story mode. One event, one mechanism (dynamite does not destroy matter, it throws it), twist ending on the car
bought in a "Whale of a Deal" sale. Hook rule (Nov 7): the full claim spoken by ~2.5 s, frame 0 mid-explosion, sound hit
on frame 0. No viewer question in the video.

## Facts (sources)
- 9 Nov 1970: a 45 ft (14 m), ~8 short ton sperm whale washed up near Florence, Oregon. Wikipedia "Exploding whale"
- Oregon's ocean shore is legally a state highway (1913, Gov. Oswald West), so the Oregon Highway Division got the job.
  Wikipedia "Exploding whale"; Oregon Encyclopedia
- Engineer George Thornton led it; the plan was to blow it into pieces small enough for scavengers (gulls) to clean up.
  Wikipedia
- Half a short ton (20 cases) of dynamite. Walter Umenhofer, a WWII veteran with explosives training who happened to
  be there, said 20 sticks would be enough; he was ignored. Wikipedia; audacy.com/connectingvets; jalopnik.com
- 12 Nov 1970, 3:45 pm: the blast threw flesh up to 800 ft (240 m); large chunks fell on the onlookers and their cars.
  Wikipedia
- The scavenger birds stayed away (scared by the noise); most of the carcass remained and was buried. Wikipedia
- Thornton to the Register-Guard: "It went just exactly right." Wikipedia
- 2020: Florence named a park "Exploding Whale Memorial Park". Wikipedia
- Umenhofer's new Oldsmobile was crushed by a falling chunk of blubber; he had bought it days earlier at a dealer's
  "Get a Whale of a Deal" promotion. Wikipedia; jalopnik.com; offbeatoregon.com
- Staging only: the jelly-and-hammer analogy, the countdown.

## Title
Oregon Blew Up a Whale. On Purpose.

Script:
Oregon blew up a whale. On purpose.
November nineteen seventy. A dead sperm whale, longer than a bus, washes up on the beach at Florence.
It weighs eight tons, and it stinks.
In Oregon, the beach is legally a highway. So the dead whale is a job for the highway department.
Their engineer, George Thornton, has a plan: blow it into tiny pieces, and let the seagulls eat them.
How much dynamite? Half a ton.
Walter Umenhofer, a war veteran who knows explosives, warns them: twenty sticks is plenty. They use twenty cases.
So Walter parks his brand new car, and the crowd steps way back.
Three. Two. One.
Then it starts raining whale.
Huge chunks of blubber land on the crowd, up to eight hundred feet away.
Wait. How does half a ton of dynamite not get rid of a whale?
You see, dynamite doesn't make things disappear. It just makes things move.
Hit a bowl of jelly with a hammer. The jelly doesn't vanish. It lands on everybody.
And the seagulls? The blast scared them all away. Most of the whale is still lying there.
The engineer told the newspaper, it went just exactly right.
Fifty years later, the town named a park after it. Exploding Whale Memorial Park.
And Walter's new car? A falling chunk of blubber crushed it.
He had bought it days earlier, in a sale called, get a whale of a deal.
