# "The Tower That Was Saved by a War" — original script v2 (2026-11-03, approved)
Format: cartoon 3D story short, > 60 s (target 63-68 s), one steady narrator, no captions.

Facts (Wikipedia, "Leaning Tower of Pisa", and Ingenia, "The stabilisation of the Leaning Tower of Pisa"):
- Construction began in 1173 (white marble bell tower of Pisa cathedral).
- In 1178 the tower began to sink after construction reached the second floor: a 3 m foundation in weak, unstable subsoil.
- Construction was then halted for the better part of a century (Pisa almost continually at war with Genoa, Lucca and
  Florence); this let the soil settle. "Otherwise, the tower would almost certainly have toppled."
- Work resumed in 1272; to compensate, upper floors were built with one side taller than the other, so the tower is curved.
- Halted again in 1278 when the Pisans were defeated by the Genoese at Meloria (Pisa lost).
- Closed to the public on 7 January 1990; tilt 5.5 degrees.
- From 1993, 870 tonnes of lead counterweights on the north (high) side straightened it slightly.
- Soil extraction under the raised end: 38 m3, 70 tonnes in total; tilt reduced by 45 cm (back to its 1838 position);
  declared stable for at least 200-300 years. Engineers deliberately did not straighten it.
Analogy: standing still on wet sand: water squeezes out under the weight and the sand firms up (consolidation of clay).

Script:
The most famous tower in Italy is only standing because its builders went off to war.
It's 1173, and the city of Pisa starts a bell tower in pure white marble.
Five years later, they reach the second floor, and one side starts sinking into the soft ground.
Then Pisa goes to war, and the builders drop their tools.
Luckily, Pisa kept going to war. Unluckily, it also kept losing.
Work stops for almost a hundred years.
When they come back, the tower still leans, so they build every new floor a little taller on the low side.
That's why it's actually curved, like a banana.
And it keeps tipping, a little more every year, until 1990, when the city has to close it.
First, engineers stacked eight hundred and seventy tonnes of lead blocks on the high side, like a giant paperweight.
It helped, a little.
Ever stood still on wet sand? The water slowly squeezes out under your feet, and the sand goes firm.
The war gave the clay under the tower a hundred years to do exactly that.
Without it, the tower would almost certainly have toppled.
So engineers did something strange. They pulled seventy tonnes of soil out from under the high side, and the tower sank back forty-five centimetres.
Then they stopped on purpose. Nobody flies to Italy to see a straight tower.
